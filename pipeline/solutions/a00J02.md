## Setup

**What the problem asks:** Given $f:[0,1]\to\mathbb{R}$ absolutely continuous with $f(0)=0$ and $f'\in L^2[0,1]$, prove that $\lim_{x\to 0^+} x^{-1/2}f(x)$ exists and find it.

**Background you need:**

- **Absolute continuity** means $f$ is the integral of its derivative: $f(x) = f(0) + \int_0^x f'(t)\,dt$. Since $f(0)=0$, we get $f(x) = \int_0^x f'(t)\,dt$.
- **Cauchy–Schwarz for integrals:** $\left|\int_0^x g\,h\right|^2 \leq \int_0^x |g|^2 \cdot \int_0^x |h|^2$.
- **Absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integral:** If $\varphi \in L^1$, then $\int_0^x |\varphi|\,dt \to 0$ as $x\to 0^+$.

---

## Key Insight

The representation $f(x) = \int_0^x f'(t)\,dt$ turns this into a Cauchy–Schwarz problem. Applying C–S with $g=1$ and $h=f'$ produces exactly the factor $x^{1/2}$ that cancels the denominator, leaving a remainder that vanishes by absolute continuity of the integral. **The limit is 0.**

---

## Proof

Since $f$ is absolutely continuous with $f(0)=0$:

$$f(x) = \int_0^x f'(t)\,dt.$$

Apply Cauchy–Schwarz:

$$|f(x)| = \left|\int_0^x 1 \cdot f'(t)\,dt\right| \leq \left(\int_0^x 1^2\,dt\right)^{1/2}\!\left(\int_0^x |f'(t)|^2\,dt\right)^{1/2} = x^{1/2}\left(\int_0^x |f'(t)|^2\,dt\right)^{1/2}.$$

Dividing both sides by $x^{1/2}$ (for $x>0$):

$$\left|\frac{f(x)}{x^{1/2}}\right| \leq \left(\int_0^x |f'(t)|^2\,dt\right)^{1/2}.$$

Since $|f'|^2 \in L^1[0,1]$ (given), absolute continuity of the Lebesgue integral gives

$$\int_0^x |f'(t)|^2\,dt \;\xrightarrow{x\to 0^+}\; 0.$$

By the squeeze theorem, $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0^+} \frac{f(x)}{x^{1/2}} = 0$. $\blacksquare$

---

## Where You'd Get Stuck

**Dead end 1: L'Hôpital's rule.** You might try $\lim \frac{f(x)}{x^{1/2}} = \lim \frac{f'(x)}{\frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}} = \lim 2x^{1/2}f'(x)$. But $f'$ is only an $L^2$ function — it need not have a pointwise limit at $0$, so this goes nowhere. L'Hôpital requires the derivative quotient to have a limit.

**Dead end 2: Trying to show the limit is nonzero.** The problem says "determine the value," which tempts you to think it might be something interesting. But the $x^{-1/2}$ rate is exactly the worst-case growth for an integral against an $L^2$ function (by C–S), so the hypothesis forces the limit to be $0$. The problem is testing whether you recognize that.

**Trap: Forgetting why $\int_0^x |f'|^2\,dt \to 0$.** Some students apply Cauchy–Schwarz correctly but then wave their hands at the last step. The precise justification is: for any $\varphi\in L^1$, the function $F(x)=\int_0^x |\varphi|\,dt$ is continuous with $F(0)=0$, so $F(x)\to 0$. Here $\varphi = |f'|^2$.

**Sharpness check:** The bound is tight. Take $f(x)=x^{1/2}$, which has $f'(x)=\frac{1}{2}x^{-1/2}$ and $\int_0^1|f'|^2 = \int_0^1 \frac{1}{4x}\,dx = +\infty$. So $f(x)/x^{1/2}=1\not\to 0$, but this $f$ violates the $L^2$ hypothesis — confirming that the square-integrability of $f'$ is doing real work.
