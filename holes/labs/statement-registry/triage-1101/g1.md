## Evidence and mathematical checks

### a98A07

The original says “a sequence of functions holomorphic on an open region containing the closed unit disk” (`problem.tex` lines 1–2). Grammatically this may denote one common region, but in standard usage it can also abbreviate an individual neighborhood for every member; the intended quantifier must be declared. The conclusion and boundary hypotheses are complete and mathematically sound because the displayed majorant is integrable at (0) after the substitution (s=-\log(t/(4\pi))), and dominated convergence gives boundary (L^1) convergence.

Lean lines 404–417 quantify a single `U` and require `∀ n, DifferentiableOn ℂ (f n) U`; lines 409–414 encode the pointwise bound and a.e. complex limit, and lines 415–417 give a holomorphic locally uniform limit on the disk. Thus there is no missing conclusion, scalar mismatch, or junk integral. If “common” is intended, the registered statement is faithful; otherwise only the neighborhood quantifier is too strong.

### a00J06

The original explicitly defines (f(z)=\sum a(n)z^n), says its radius is (1), and asks to exclude analytic continuation to a disk centered at the complex point (1) (`problem.tex` lines 1–5). This is Pringsheim's theorem in this special form and is sound.

Lean instead defines `p : FormalMultilinearSeries ℝ ℝ ℝ` and `f ... (z : ℝ) : ℝ` (lines 57–62), then concludes only `¬ ∃ ρ, 0 < ρ ∧ AnalyticAt ℝ (f a) 1` (lines 93–96). The proof deliberately uses the library convention that a nonsummable `tsum` is zero to the right of (1) (lines 86–101). That is a legitimate device for the stronger real-germ obstruction, but it is not the original complex function outside its disk and the complex-to-real restriction implication is absent from the statement. A thin complex wrapper should be small if the analytic restriction bridge is available.

### a02J07

The source requires a piecewise smooth differentiable path, (gamma(0)=\pi), and (gamma^{-1}([0,\infty))=\{0\}), then asks for both strict inequalities (`problem.tex` lines 1–4). It is sound: a continuous argument on the slit plane takes values in ((0,2\pi)), while the one-sided initial argument is (0) or (2\pi); integrating (d\log z) gives a nonzero imaginary difference of magnitude below (2\pi).

Lean's `SlitPath` has the correct start and avoidance (lines 125–131), but demands pointwise differentiability throughout ((0,1]) and separately assumes `IntervalIntegrable (fun t => deriv γ t / γ t)` (lines 132–136). The theorem does conclude exactly `0 < |...im| ∧ |...im| < 2 * π` (lines 297–298). The extra integrability is semantic hygiene rather than the core result, but a faithful wrapper must derive it from a precise piecewise-smooth hypothesis.

### a01A11

The source's part (c) says `zf(z)` although only (g) was defined (`problem.tex` lines 1–13); it becomes sound under the evident convention (f=g), with limit (-m(K)). The Markdown copy preserves that typo. Positive measure also makes (K) nonempty, so that separate assumption is harmlessly derivable.

Lean defines the correct Cauchy transform (lines 19–23), proves differentiability off the embedded (K) (lines 57–58), and proves (z g(z)\to-m(K)) (lines 134–136). But `cauchyTransform_not_entire` assumes `h_diff`, `h_decay`, and `h_nonzero` (lines 246–251). In particular decay and nonzeroness are essentially all the Liouville contradiction; no theorem assembles them from compactness and positive measure. Deriving decay from the line-134 asymptotic and nonzeroness from its nonzero limit should be a medium-sized closure, not a new theory.

### a95A06

Part (b), literally printed, is false. Let

\[
U=\Delta\cup B(2,1/10).
\]

The two open disks are disjoint, (U) is open and properly contains (Delta), and define (F=f) on (Delta) and (F=0) on (B(2,1/10)). This piecewise definition is analytic on (U) and agrees with (f) throughout (Delta), contradicting lines 9–11 of `problem.tex`. The smallest correction is that (U) be a connected open set/region (or an equivalent no-irrelevant-components condition).

Lean makes exactly that repair with `IsConnected U` at lines 403–407. It also defines `lacunarySeries` as a sum over all naturals (lines 52–58), although the source starts at (n=1) (`problem.tex` line 3); this adds the (n=0) term (z). Continuity and analyticity are proved at lines 114–115 and 152–153, and the connected natural-boundary result at lines 403–407. Adding/subtracting (z) preserves the natural boundary, but the registered function is not literally the source's function.

### a97A07

The exact-degree assertion is false. Take (n=2), (z_1=0), (z_2=1/2), (gamma(t)=e^{2\pi i t}), and (f(z)=1). Both nodes are distinct and inside the Jordan curve and (f) is entire. The displayed integral is the interpolation polynomial taking value (1) at both nodes; explicitly the degree-(le1) interpolant is (P(z)=1), of degree (0), not (n-1=1). Equivalently Cauchy's formula applied to the divided-difference integrand yields that same constant. Thus `problem.tex` lines 7–8 must say “degree at most (n-1).”

Lean correctly concludes `P_poly.natDegree ≤ n - 1` (lines 354–363), but assumes `AnalyticOn ℂ f univ` and hard-codes the centered circle of radius (R) (lines 357–362). The source only asks that (f) be analytic inside and on an arbitrary Jordan curve (`problem.tex` lines 1–4). After correcting the source degree, the remaining geometric/local-analytic generalization is large.

### a98J03

As printed, the denominator is ((x-t)+y^2), not ((x-t)^2+y^2) (`problem.tex` lines 1–3), and the statement is false even as a definition. Take (y=1), (f=\mathbf 1_{[0,1]}in L^2(\mathbb R)), and any (x\in[-1,0]). Then the pole (t=x+1) lies in ([0,1]), and

\[
\int_0^1 \frac{dt}{x-t+1}
\]

is not Lebesgue integrable (the absolute integral diverges logarithmically, and the positive and negative parts are both infinite for interior poles). Hence (g_y(x)) is not defined by the asserted integral on a positive-measure set, so (a) already fails. The smallest correction is ((x-t)^2+y^2).

Lean makes that correction in `apmPoissonKernel`/`apmPoissonIntegral` (lines 573–577) and gives the bounded real-linear operators and convergence at lines 839–846. It is explicitly `Lp ℝ 2`, so it does not cover complex-valued (L^2), a convention the source leaves unstated.

### a96A04

The original is internally ambiguous rather than a single well-typed theorem: it starts “(f\in L^2(\mathbb R))” but its formula and norms use (mathbb R^n) (`problem.tex` lines 1–13); it also writes ((x-y)^2) for vectors. The prefactor as line-broken TeX, `(1/4π t)^{n/2}`, should presumably mean ((4\pi t)^{-n/2}), but parentheses should make that explicit. Under either consistent one-dimensional or (n)-dimensional standard heat-kernel reading, all three requested conclusions are sound.

Lean chooses the one-dimensional real kernel `(4 * π * t) ^ (-(1 : ℝ) / 2)` (lines 45–51). It proves genuine (C^\infty), not analytic regularity, at lines 695–705, the (L^2) contraction at lines 742–751, and the positive-time (L^2) limit at lines 918–922. Thus the Lean mathematics is good but cannot decide the source's dimension. The smallest source correction is the consistent (n=1) reading; choosing the likely intended (mathbb R^n) problem requires a substantial generalization.

### a02J03

The source says “if (f\in L^1(\mathbb R^n)) and (k>0)” and immediately writes `lim_{k→∞}` (`problem.tex` lines 4–5), so (k) is simultaneously fixed and bound by the limit. Rename it (say (lambda\to\infty)). It also omits (M>0) and does not say whether the integrals of merely measurable (arphi) are finite Lebesgue integrals; those conventions matter. With (M>0), local integrability, and the renamed scale parameter, the standard rectangle-density argument makes the theorem sound.

Lean's main theorem is entirely one-dimensional and real-valued: `φ f : ℝ → ℝ`, local integrability, interval bounds, and `M > 0` appear at lines 406–411. The source quantifies over every (n)-dimensional rectangle (`problem.tex` lines 1–3), not just intervals. The added local integrability prevents an undefined/totalized integral from satisfying the premise spuriously, but the finite-dimensional rectangle approximation and scaling argument are absent. This is the clearest genuine theory/generalization gap in the group.
