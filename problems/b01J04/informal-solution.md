# APM b01J04: Simultaneous diagonalization of commuting matrices, and A⁻¹ as a polynomial in A

## Why It's Hard

**Part (a)** hides a trap that the literal statement glosses over. The forward implication (simultaneously diagonalizable $\Rightarrow$ commute) is a one-liner, but the converse is the substantive theorem — and **commuting alone is *not* sufficient**. The matrix $A=B=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$ commutes with itself yet is not diagonalizable, so no $T$ diagonalizes it. The hypothesis that makes the converse true is that $A$ and $B$ are *each individually diagonalizable* — which is automatically necessary, since any matrix conjugate to a diagonal one is diagonalizable. So the precise equivalence is
$$
\exists\,T:\ TAT^{-1},\,TBT^{-1}\ \text{diagonal}\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad AB=BA\ \textbf{and}\ A,B\ \text{each diagonalizable.}
$$
The real mathematical content of the converse is two layered facts that are easy to overlook: commuting forces $B$ to **preserve each eigenspace of $A$**, and the restriction of a diagonalizable operator to an invariant subspace is **again diagonalizable**.

**Part (b)** is surprising because inversion looks like a *rational* operation — Cramer's rule puts $\det A$ in a denominator — yet the inverse turns out to be a *polynomial* in $A$. The difficulty is recognizing that **Cayley–Hamilton** plus **nonsingularity** ($\Leftrightarrow$ nonzero constant term of the characteristic polynomial) is exactly what converts "$A$ satisfies a degree-$n$ relation" into "$I$ lies in the span of $A,\dots,A^n$."

## The Key Insight

**(a)** Commuting is precisely the statement that $B$ maps each eigenspace $E_\lambda=\ker(A-\lambda I)$ into itself. Inside $E_\lambda$, $A$ is *already* the scalar $\lambda$, so **any** basis diagonalizes $A$ there — in particular a basis of $B$-eigenvectors, which exists because $B|_{E_\lambda}$ is diagonalizable. Assembling these bases over all $\lambda$ yields a basis of *common* eigenvectors; the change-of-basis matrix simultaneously diagonalizes.

**(b)** By Cayley–Hamilton, $A$ satisfies $p(A)=A^n+\dots+c_1A+c_0I=0$ with $c_0=\pm\det A\ne 0$. Solving for the constant term, $c_0 I=-A(A^{n-1}+\dots+c_1I)$, so
$$
A^{-1}=-\tfrac{1}{c_0}\big(A^{n-1}+c_{n-1}A^{n-2}+\cdots+c_1 I\big)=q(A).
$$
The inverse lives in the commutative algebra $k[A]$ generated by $A$ — nonsingularity is exactly the nonvanishing of $c_0$ that makes the division legal.

## Complete Proof

### Part (a): simultaneous diagonalizability $\Leftrightarrow$ commuting (for diagonalizable $A,B$)

**($\Rightarrow$) Simultaneously diagonalizable implies commuting.** Suppose $TAT^{-1}=D_1$ and $TBT^{-1}=D_2$ with $D_1,D_2$ diagonal. Diagonal matrices commute, so $D_1D_2=D_2D_1$, i.e.
$$
T(AB)T^{-1}=D_1D_2=D_2D_1=T(BA)T^{-1}\ \Longrightarrow\ AB=BA.
$$
(Also each of $A=T^{-1}D_1T$, $B=T^{-1}D_2T$ is then diagonalizable — recording the necessary extra hypothesis for the converse.)

**($\Leftarrow$) Commuting (plus diagonalizability) implies simultaneously diagonalizable.** Assume $AB=BA$ and that $A,B$ are each diagonalizable over $\mathbb{C}$.

*Step 1 — $B$ preserves the eigenspaces of $A$.* Since $A$ is diagonalizable, $\mathbb{C}^n=\bigoplus_{\lambda}E_\lambda$, where $E_\lambda=\ker(A-\lambda I)$ ranges over the distinct eigenvalues $\lambda$ of $A$. For $v\in E_\lambda$, commutativity gives
$$
A(Bv)=B(Av)=B(\lambda v)=\lambda(Bv),
$$
so $Bv\in E_\lambda$. Thus each $E_\lambda$ is $B$-invariant.

*Step 2 — $B$ restricted to $E_\lambda$ is diagonalizable.* Because $B$ is diagonalizable, its minimal polynomial $m_B(x)$ is a product of **distinct** linear factors. The minimal polynomial of the restriction $B|_{E_\lambda}$ divides $m_B$, hence is also a product of distinct linear factors — so $B|_{E_\lambda}$ is diagonalizable. Therefore $E_\lambda$ has a basis $\mathcal{B}_\lambda$ consisting of eigenvectors of $B$.

*Step 3 — assemble a common eigenbasis.* Every vector of $\mathcal{B}_\lambda$ lies in $E_\lambda$, so it is also an eigenvector of $A$ (with eigenvalue $\lambda$). Hence each $v\in\mathcal{B}_\lambda$ is a **common** eigenvector of $A$ and $B$. The union $\mathcal{B}=\bigcup_\lambda \mathcal{B}_\lambda$ is a basis of $\mathbb{C}^n=\bigoplus_\lambda E_\lambda$ consisting of common eigenvectors. Let $S$ be the matrix whose columns are the vectors of $\mathcal{B}$ and put $T=S^{-1}$. Then $TAT^{-1}$ and $TBT^{-1}$ are both diagonal (each basis vector is an eigenvector of both). $\qquad\checkmark$

*(Caveat made precise: the converse genuinely needs diagonalizability of $A$ and $B$. With $A=B=\big(\begin{smallmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\big)$ we have $AB=BA$ but neither is diagonalizable, so no simultaneous diagonalization exists — the bare "commuting" hypothesis is insufficient, and the iff should be read with "each diagonalizable" included, as it is forced by the left-hand side.)*

### Part (b): $A^{-1}$ is a polynomial in $A$

Let $A\in k^{n\times n}$ be nonsingular and let
$$
p(x)=\det(xI-A)=x^n+c_{n-1}x^{n-1}+\cdots+c_1x+c_0\in k[x]
$$
be its characteristic polynomial. Its constant term is
$$
c_0=p(0)=\det(-A)=(-1)^n\det A\ \ne\ 0,
$$
because $A$ is nonsingular; and $c_0\in k$ is therefore invertible in the field $k$.

By the **Cayley–Hamilton theorem**, $p(A)=0$:
$$
A^n+c_{n-1}A^{n-1}+\cdots+c_1A+c_0I=0 .
$$
Move the constant term to one side and factor out $A$:
$$
c_0 I=-\big(A^n+c_{n-1}A^{n-1}+\cdots+c_1A\big)=-A\big(A^{n-1}+c_{n-1}A^{n-2}+\cdots+c_1 I\big).
$$
Dividing by the nonzero scalar $c_0$,
$$
I=A\cdot\Big(-\tfrac{1}{c_0}\big(A^{n-1}+c_{n-1}A^{n-2}+\cdots+c_1 I\big)\Big),
$$
and since this factor commutes with $A$ (it is a polynomial in $A$), it is a two-sided inverse. Hence $A^{-1}=q(A)$ where
$$
\boxed{\,q(x)=-\tfrac{1}{c_0}\big(x^{n-1}+c_{n-1}x^{n-2}+\cdots+c_1\big)\in k[x].\,}\qquad\blacksquare
$$

### Remark — both parts live in the algebra $k[A]$

The two parts are complementary facets of operators acting on $k^n$. Part (b) says $k[A]$, the commutative subalgebra of $k^{n\times n}$ generated by $A$, already contains $A^{-1}$ — so inverses are "free" once you have powers; equivalently $A$ is a unit in the finite-dimensional algebra $k[A]\cong k[x]/(m_A)$ precisely when $x\nmid m_A(x)$, i.e. when $0$ is not an eigenvalue. Part (a) is about *pairs* of operators: commuting diagonalizable matrices generate a commutative algebra that is **itself diagonalizable** (simultaneously), the simplest instance of the spectral theorem for commuting families. The throughline is that commuting operators can be understood one eigenspace at a time, and the obstruction to diagonalizing — repeated minimal-polynomial roots in (a), a zero eigenvalue in (b) — is exactly what the hypotheses (diagonalizability; nonsingularity) rule out.
