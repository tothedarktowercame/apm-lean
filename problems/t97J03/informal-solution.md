# APM t97J03: A self-map of the boundary torus that does not extend over the solid torus

## Why It's Hard

The question is whether the boundary map `f(z,w)=(z,w^3)` is the restriction of some map defined on the whole
solid torus `Y=D^2\times S^1`. Extension problems are governed by `\pi_1`, and the difficulty is locating the
*right* obstruction loop. The solid torus collapses one of the two boundary circles:

- The **meridian** `\mu=\partial D^2\times\{pt\}` **bounds a disk** in `Y` (the meridian disk `D^2\times\{pt\}`),
  so it becomes null-homotopic once included into `Y`. Hence *any* continuous map out of `Y` must send `\mu`
  to a null-homotopic loop.
- But `f` sends `\mu` to itself, `f_*(\mu)=\mu`, which is **not** null-homotopic in `T^2`. So `f` keeps alive
  a loop that any extension is forced to kill — contradiction.

The trap is the conspicuous "`3`-fold" in `w\mapsto w^3`: that only affects the **longitude** `\lambda`, which
*survives* in `\pi_1(Y)=\mathbb Z` and poses no obstruction. The genuine obstruction is the meridian, which
`f` leaves intact (`f` is the identity in the `z`-direction). Recognizing that the longitude is a red herring
and the meridian is decisive is the key.

## The Key Insight

`Y=D^2\times S^1` deformation-retracts to its core circle, so `\pi_1(Y)=\mathbb Z` (generated by the
longitude), and the inclusion `i:T^2=\partial Y\hookrightarrow Y` sends the meridian `\mu` to `0` (it bounds
the meridian disk). If `F:Y\to T^2` extended `f`, then `F\circ i=f`, so on `\pi_1`,
$$
f_*(\mu) = (F\circ i)_*(\mu) = F_*\big(i_*(\mu)\big) = F_*(0) = 0 .
$$
But `f(z,w)=(z,w^3)` fixes the meridian direction, so `f_*(\mu)=\mu\ne0` in `\pi_1(T^2)=\mathbb Z^2`. The
contradiction `0=\mu\ne0` shows no such `F` exists.

## Complete Proof

Write the solid torus as `Y=D^2\times S^1` (so `\dim Y=3`) with boundary `T^2=\partial Y=\partial D^2\times
S^1=S^1\times S^1`. On `T^2` use coordinates `(z,w)` with `z\in\partial D^2=S^1` (the *meridian* direction) and
`w\in S^1` (the *longitude*/core direction), and let `i:T^2\hookrightarrow Y` be the inclusion. The given map
is `f:T^2\to T^2`, `f(z,w)=(z,w^3)`. Suppose, for contradiction, that there is a continuous `F:Y\to T^2` with
`F|_{T^2}=f`, i.e. `F\circ i=f`.

### Fundamental groups

Mark base points compatibly. The two standard generating loops of `\pi_1(T^2)=\mathbb Z^2` are:
$$
\mu = \big[\,\theta\mapsto(e^{i\theta},w_0)\,\big] \ (\text{meridian}), \qquad \lambda = \big[\,\theta\mapsto(z_0,e^{i\theta})\,\big] \ (\text{longitude}),
$$
so `\pi_1(T^2)=\mathbb Z\langle\mu\rangle\oplus\mathbb Z\langle\lambda\rangle`.

**The meridian dies in `Y`.** `Y=D^2\times S^1` deformation-retracts onto its core circle `\{0\}\times S^1`,
so `\pi_1(Y)\cong\mathbb Z`, generated by the longitude. The meridian `\mu=\partial D^2\times\{w_0\}` is the
boundary of the **meridian disk** `D^2\times\{w_0\}\subset Y`, a genuine disk in `Y`; hence `\mu` is
null-homotopic in `Y`:
$$
i_*(\mu) = 0 \quad\text{in } \pi_1(Y).
$$

**`f` keeps the meridian alive.** Apply `f` to the meridian loop: `f(e^{i\theta},w_0)=(e^{i\theta},w_0^3)`,
which as `\theta` runs over `[0,2\pi]` traces the meridian circle once (at fixed second coordinate `w_0^3`).
Therefore
$$
f_*(\mu) = \mu = (1,0) \ne 0 \quad\text{in } \pi_1(T^2).
$$
(The cubing affects only the longitude: `f_*(\lambda)=3\lambda`. But `\lambda` survives in `\pi_1(Y)` —
`i_*(\lambda)` is the generator — so it is *not* where the obstruction lives.)

### The contradiction

By functoriality of `\pi_1`, the relation `F\circ i=f` gives `F_*\circ i_*=f_*`. Evaluate on `\mu`:
$$
f_*(\mu) = (F\circ i)_*(\mu) = F_*\big(i_*(\mu)\big) = F_*(0) = 0,
$$
using `i_*(\mu)=0` and that `F_*` is a homomorphism. But we computed `f_*(\mu)=\mu\ne0`. Hence
$$
0 = f_*(\mu) = \mu \ne 0 \quad\text{in } \pi_1(T^2),
$$
a contradiction. Therefore no continuous `F:Y\to T^2` extends `f`. ∎

*(Geometric restatement: if `F` extended `f`, restricting `F` to the meridian disk `D=D^2\times\{w_0\}` would
give a continuous map `F|_D:D\to T^2` of a disk whose boundary loop is `F|_{\partial D}=f|_\mu`. So `f|_\mu`
would extend over the disk `D`, i.e. be null-homotopic in `T^2`. But `f|_\mu` represents `\mu\ne0` in
`\pi_1(T^2)`, which is not null-homotopic — contradiction.)*

**Remark.** The obstruction is entirely about which boundary curve **bounds** in `Y`. Filling the solid torus
kills exactly the meridian `\mu` (it caps off with the meridian disk), so the necessary condition for a
boundary map `f:T^2\to T^2` to extend over `Y` is `f_*(\mu)=0` in `\pi_1(T^2)` — an extension `F` forces
`f_*(\mu)=F_*(i_*\mu)=F_*(0)=0`. Our `f` fails this, since `f_*(\mu)=\mu\ne0` (it is the identity in the
meridian direction). By contrast, a map that *collapses* the meridian, e.g. `(z,w)\mapsto(1,w)`, has
`f_*(\mu)=0` and does extend — via `(d,w)\mapsto(1,w)` over all of `Y`. The `3`-fold cover of the *longitude*
is irrelevant to extendability: `\lambda` survives in `\pi_1(Y)=\mathbb Z`, so `f_*(\lambda)=3\lambda` poses
no obstruction. It is the untouched meridian — the curve that `Y` fills in but `f` keeps nontrivial — that
obstructs.
