Informal proof

**Why it's hard:** The hypothesis is about the *Taylor coefficients* $a_n = f^{(n)}(0)/n!$, while the conclusion is geometric (about the domain $D$). The bridge is the radius of convergence formula combined with analytic continuation, but there's a subtle point: the radius of convergence is determined by the $\limsup$ of $|a_n|^{1/n}$ (Cauchy-Hadamard), not by $\limsup |a_n|$.

**The key insight:** The hypothesis $\limsup |a_n| > 0$ (where $a_n = f^{(n)}(0)/n!$) implies the Taylor coefficients don't decay to zero, and in particular $\limsup |a_n|^{1/n} \geq 1$. By Cauchy-Hadamard, the radius of convergence of the Taylor series at $0$ is at most $1$. If $D$ contained the closed unit disk, then by analyticity, $f$ would extend with radius of convergence strictly greater than $1$ (since the closed disk lies in the open set $D$), contradicting $R \leq 1$.

**Proof:**

Set $a_n = f^{(n)}(0)/n!$. Let $L = \limsup_{n \to \infty} |a_n| > 0$.

*Step 1: $\limsup |a_n|^{1/n} \geq 1$.*

Pick any $0 < L' < L$. By definition of $\limsup$, there are infinitely many $n$ with $|a_n| > L'$. For each such $n$, $|a_n|^{1/n} > (L')^{1/n} \to 1$ as $n \to \infty$. So for any $\varepsilon > 0$, all but finitely many of these $n$ satisfy $|a_n|^{1/n} > 1 - \varepsilon$. Hence $\limsup_n |a_n|^{1/n} \geq 1 - \varepsilon$. Since $\varepsilon > 0$ was arbitrary:
$$\limsup_{n \to \infty} |a_n|^{1/n} \geq 1$$

*Step 2: The radius of convergence $R$ of $f$ at $0$ satisfies $R \leq 1$.*

By the Cauchy-Hadamard formula:
$$\frac{1}{R} = \limsup_{n \to \infty} |a_n|^{1/n} \geq 1$$

Therefore $R \leq 1$.

*Step 3: If $D$ contains the closed unit disk, then $R > 1$.*

Suppose for contradiction that $D \supset \bar{D} = \{z : |z| \leq 1\}$. Since $D$ is open and $\bar{D}$ is compact, there exists $r > 1$ such that the open disk $\{z : |z| < r\}$ is contained in $D$. (Because the distance function $d(\cdot, \mathbb{C} \setminus D)$ is continuous and strictly positive on the compact set $\bar{D}$, it attains a positive minimum $\delta > 0$; taking $r = 1 + \delta/2$ gives an open disk of radius $r > 1$ contained in $D$.)

Since $f$ is analytic on the open disk $\{|z| < r\}$ and $r > 1$, the Taylor series of $f$ at $0$ converges on this disk (by the standard fact that the Taylor series of an analytic function converges in any disk on which the function is analytic). So $R \geq r > 1$.

This contradicts $R \leq 1$ from Step 2. Therefore $D$ cannot contain the closed unit disk. ∎

**What connects:** This is the standard duality between coefficient growth and analyticity domain, encoded in Cauchy-Hadamard. The hypothesis $\limsup |a_n| > 0$ is weaker than the more common $\limsup |a_n|^{1/n} \geq 1$, but it implies it (since $|a_n| \geq L'$ for infinitely many $n$ forces $|a_n|^{1/n} \geq (L')^{1/n} \to 1$). The key mathematical fact is that the Taylor series of $f$ at $0$ converges in the *largest open disk centered at $0$ contained in the domain of analyticity*, so the radius of convergence is the distance from $0$ to the nearest singularity. If $0$'s domain of analyticity contains the closed unit disk, the nearest singularity is at distance $> 1$, giving $R > 1$. The contrapositive form of this statement gives Hadamard's lacunary series criterion and is the foundation of natural boundary results.
