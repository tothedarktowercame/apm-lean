# APM b96J03: Nilpotent, solvable, and a presented group that is neither

## Why It's Hard

Parts (a) and (b) are bookkeeping once the definitions are in hand; the difficulty is
concentrated in part (c), and it is a difficulty of reading rather than of technique. The
relations $ab = ba$ and $ac = ca$ tell us how $a$ interacts with $b$ and with $c$ — and they
say nothing whatever about how $b$ and $c$ interact. It is tempting to supply a missing
relation and conclude that $G$ is a semidirect product of cyclic groups, hence solvable.
That reading is wrong. With no relation tying $b$ to $c$, the subgroup they generate is as
free as a group generated by an element of order $3$ and an element of order $7$ can be,
and such a group is very far from solvable. The whole of part (c) is the discipline of
using only the relations that are actually written down.

## The Key Insight

To prove a group is *not* solvable it suffices to exhibit one non-solvable quotient, since
quotients of solvable groups are solvable. A quotient is just a homomorphism, and by von
Dyck's theorem a homomorphism out of a presented group is specified by choosing images of
the generators that satisfy the relations — nothing more. So we may send $a \mapsto e$,
which makes both commutation relations hold for free, and send $b$ and $c$ to any elements
of order dividing $3$ and $7$ in any group we like. Choosing a $3$-cycle and a $7$-cycle in
$A_7$ makes the image all of $A_7$, which is simple and non-abelian, hence not solvable.
Nilpotence then fails automatically, by part (b)(i).

## Complete Proof

### (a) Definitions

**Nilpotent.** A group $G$ is *nilpotent* if it has a **central series**: a finite chain of
normal subgroups of $G$

$$1 = G_0 \le G_1 \le \cdots \le G_n = G, \qquad G_i \trianglelefteq G,$$

such that $G_{i+1}/G_i \le Z(G/G_i)$ for each $i$; equivalently, $[G_{i+1}, G] \le G_i$ for
each $i$. Equivalently, the lower central series defined by $\gamma_1(G) = G$ and
$\gamma_{i+1}(G) = [\gamma_i(G), G]$ reaches the trivial subgroup after finitely many steps.
The least such $n$ is the *nilpotency class*.

**Solvable.** A group $G$ is *solvable* if it has a **subnormal series with abelian
quotients**: a finite chain

$$1 = H_0 \trianglelefteq H_1 \trianglelefteq \cdots \trianglelefteq H_n = G$$

with each $H_{i+1}/H_i$ abelian. Equivalently, the derived series defined by
$G^{(0)} = G$ and $G^{(i+1)} = [G^{(i)}, G^{(i)}]$ reaches the trivial subgroup after finitely
many steps.

### (b)(i) A nilpotent group is solvable — **true**

**Proof.** Let $G$ be nilpotent with central series $1 = G_0 \le G_1 \le \cdots \le G_n = G$.
Each $G_i$ is normal in $G$, hence in particular $G_i \trianglelefteq G_{i+1}$, so the chain is
subnormal. It remains to see that each quotient $G_{i+1}/G_i$ is abelian. By the defining
property, $G_{i+1}/G_i$ is contained in the centre of $G/G_i$; a subgroup of the centre of a
group is abelian, since its elements commute with everything in $G/G_i$ and in particular
with one another. So the chain is a subnormal series with abelian quotients, and $G$ is
solvable. $\blacksquare$

### (b)(ii) A solvable group is nilpotent — **false**

**Counterexample.** Take $G = S_3$, the symmetric group on three letters.

*$S_3$ is solvable:* the chain $1 \trianglelefteq A_3 \trianglelefteq S_3$ is subnormal, and both
quotients are abelian — $A_3 \cong C_3$ is cyclic, and $S_3/A_3 \cong C_2$ is cyclic.

*$S_3$ is not nilpotent:* the centre of $S_3$ is trivial. Indeed a non-identity element of
$S_3$ is either a transposition or a $3$-cycle; the transposition $(1\,2)$ does not commute
with $(1\,3)$, and the $3$-cycle $(1\,2\,3)$ does not commute with $(1\,2)$. Now, every
nontrivial nilpotent group has nontrivial centre: with a central series as above, take the
least $i$ with $G_i \ne 1$; then $i \ge 1$ and $[G_i, G] \le G_{i-1} = 1$, so the nontrivial
subgroup $G_i$ lies in $Z(G)$. Since $Z(S_3) = 1$ while $S_3 \ne 1$, the group $S_3$ is not
nilpotent. $\blacksquare$

### (c) The presented group $G$

Let

$$G = \langle a, b, c \mid a^2 = b^3 = c^7 = e,\ ab = ba,\ ac = ca \rangle .$$

**Claim.** $G$ is neither solvable nor nilpotent.

**Step 1: Read the presentation carefully.** The relations impose orders on $a$, $b$, $c$
and make $a$ commute with $b$ and with $c$. There is **no** relation between $b$ and $c$. So
$a$ is central, and nothing constrains the subgroup $\langle b, c\rangle$ beyond the orders of
its two generators.

**Step 2: Construct a homomorphism onto $A_7$.** Work in the alternating group $A_7$ and
put

$$\sigma = (1\,2\,3\,4\,5\,6\,7), \qquad \tau = (1\,2\,3).$$

Both are even permutations — a $k$-cycle has sign $(-1)^{k-1}$, so a $7$-cycle and a
$3$-cycle are both even — hence $\sigma, \tau \in A_7$. Define a map on generators by

$$a \mapsto e, \qquad b \mapsto \tau, \qquad c \mapsto \sigma .$$

Every defining relation is satisfied by these images: $e^2 = e$; $\tau^3 = e$ since $\tau$ is a
$3$-cycle; $\sigma^7 = e$ since $\sigma$ is a $7$-cycle; and both commutation relations hold
because the image of $a$ is the identity, which commutes with everything. By von Dyck's
theorem the assignment extends to a group homomorphism $\varphi : G \to A_7$.

**Step 3: The homomorphism is onto.** Let $H = \varphi(G) = \langle \tau, \sigma \rangle \le A_7$.
Since $\sigma$ is a $7$-cycle, $H$ acts transitively on $\{1, \dots, 7\}$. A transitive group of
prime degree is primitive: the size of a block of imprimitivity divides the degree $7$, so
blocks have size $1$ or $7$, which are the trivial ones. Thus $H$ is a primitive
permutation group of degree $7$ containing the $3$-cycle $\tau$. By Jordan's theorem, a
primitive subgroup of $S_n$ containing a $3$-cycle contains $A_n$; hence $A_7 \le H$. As
$H \le A_7$ by Step 2, we get $H = A_7$, so $\varphi$ is surjective.

**Step 4: $G$ is not solvable.** Suppose it were. Solvability passes to quotients: if
$1 = H_0 \trianglelefteq \cdots \trianglelefteq H_n = G$ has abelian quotients and $N \trianglelefteq G$, then the
images $H_iN/N$ form a subnormal series of $G/N$ whose successive quotients are
homomorphic images of the $H_{i+1}/H_i$, hence abelian. So $G/\ker\varphi \cong A_7$ would be
solvable. But $A_7$ is a non-abelian simple group, so its only subnormal series is
$1 \trianglelefteq A_7$, whose single quotient $A_7$ is not abelian; hence $A_7$ is not solvable.
This contradiction shows $G$ is not solvable.

**Step 5: $G$ is not nilpotent.** By part (b)(i), every nilpotent group is solvable. Since
$G$ is not solvable, it is not nilpotent. $\blacksquare$

**Remark on the structure of $G$.** More can be said, though it is not needed above. Since
$a$ is central of order $2$ and is subject to no other relation, and since $b$ and $c$ are
subject to no relation with each other, the presentation splits as

$$G \cong C_2 \times (C_3 * C_7),$$

the direct factor $C_2$ generated by $a$ and the free product $C_3 * C_7$ generated by $b$
and $c$. A free product of two nontrivial groups, at least one of order greater than $2$,
contains a nonabelian free subgroup of rank $2$, which gives a second and more structural
proof that $G$ is not solvable. The finite quotient used in Steps 2–4 is preferred here
because it is elementary and verifiable by direct computation.

## What Connects

The three parts sit on one axis. Nilpotence is strictly stronger than solvability, the gap
being visible already at order $6$ ($S_3$), and the standard certificate for the gap is the
centre: nilpotent groups have nontrivial centre and iterated central quotients that
terminate, whereas solvable groups need only abelian layers. Part (c) then illustrates the
standard technique for negative results about presented groups: rather than analysing the
group directly, map it onto something whose failure is known. The technique is only
available because presentations are free objects — any assignment of generators
respecting the relations extends — which is exactly the content of von Dyck's theorem and
the reason presentations are useful at all.
