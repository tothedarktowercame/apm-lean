# APM b97A03: x^6+3 — a radical extension that is its own splitting field, with Galois group S₃

## Why It's Hard

The surprise in this problem is that $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$, generated by a *single*
root of $x^6+3$, is already the full splitting field — so a degree-$6$ extension
defined by a pure radical turns out to be **Galois**, with the non-abelian group
$S_3$. The reason is a small miracle of bookkeeping: $\alpha^3$ is a square root
of $-3$, and $\zeta_6=\tfrac{1+\sqrt{-3}}{2}$, so the sixth root $\alpha$ secretly
carries the sixth root of unity needed to produce all the other roots
$\alpha\zeta_6^k$. Contrast $x^3-2$, where $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]{2})$ is *not*
normal and you must adjoin $\zeta_3$ separately. The traps are: (a) remembering
Eisenstein at $p=3$; (b) resisting the assumption that a one-root radical field
can't be normal — here it is, precisely because $\zeta_6\in\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$;
(c) deciding $\mathbb{Z}/6$ vs $S_3$ by actually checking commutativity of two
automorphisms; and (d) giving a *complete* subfield list, which means matching
subfields to the **six subgroups of $S_3$** and exhibiting each fixed field
concretely.

## The Key Insight

Write $\zeta=\zeta_6=e^{i\pi/3}$ (a primitive $6$th root of unity, with
$\zeta^3=-1$ and $\zeta+\zeta^{-1}=1$). The six roots of $x^6+3$ are
$\alpha\zeta^k$, $k=0,\dots,5$. The single structural fact

$$
\alpha^3=\sqrt{-3}\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
\zeta=\frac{1+\alpha^3}{2}\in\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)
$$

forces $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ to contain every root, so it **is** the splitting
field: Galois of degree $6$. Each $\sigma\in G$ is fixed by $\sigma(\alpha)=
\alpha\zeta^k$, and then $\sigma(\zeta)=\zeta^{(-1)^k}$ (because
$\sigma(\zeta)=\tfrac{1+\alpha^3\zeta^{3k}}2$ and $\zeta^{3k}=(-1)^k$). The two
generators
$$
\tau=\sigma_2:\ \alpha\mapsto\alpha\zeta^2,\ \zeta\mapsto\zeta\ (\text{order }3),
\qquad
\sigma=\sigma_1:\ \alpha\mapsto\alpha\zeta,\ \zeta\mapsto\zeta^{-1}\ (\text{order }2)
$$
satisfy $\sigma\tau\sigma^{-1}=\tau^{-1}$, so $G\cong S_3$. Subfields then
correspond to the six subgroups of $S_3$: $\mathbb{Q}$, the quadratic
$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$, three cubics $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha^2\zeta_3^{\,j})$, and
$\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$.

## Complete Proof

### Part (a): $f(x)=x^6+3$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$

Apply **Eisenstein's criterion at the prime $p=3$**. The coefficients are
$1,0,0,0,0,0,3$. Then $3\mid$ every coefficient except the leading one,
$3\nmid 1$, and $3^2=9\nmid 3$ (the constant term). Hence $f$ is irreducible over
$\mathbb{Q}$. $\quad\checkmark$

### Part (b): $f$ splits over $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$

Let $\alpha\in\mathbb{C}$ be a root, so $\alpha^6=-3$. Let $\zeta=e^{i\pi/3}$ be a
primitive $6$th root of unity. For each $k$,
$(\alpha\zeta^k)^6=\alpha^6\zeta^{6k}=-3$, so the six **distinct** numbers
$\alpha,\alpha\zeta,\dots,\alpha\zeta^5$ are exactly the roots of $f$; thus
$f(x)=\prod_{k=0}^5\bigl(x-\alpha\zeta^k\bigr)$ and the splitting field of $f$ is
$\mathbb{Q}(\alpha,\zeta)$.

It therefore suffices to show $\zeta\in\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$. Since
$(\alpha^3)^2=\alpha^6=-3$, the element $\alpha^3\in\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ is a square
root of $-3$. A primitive $6$th root of unity is
$\zeta=\tfrac{1+\sqrt{-3}}2$, so taking $\sqrt{-3}=\alpha^3$,
$$
\zeta=\frac{1+\alpha^3}{2}\in\mathbb{Q}(\alpha).
$$
(Indeed $\bigl(\tfrac{1+\alpha^3}2\bigr)$ has $\zeta^3=-1$ and $\zeta+\bar\zeta=1$,
the defining data of $\zeta_6$.) Consequently every root
$\alpha\zeta^k\in\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$, and $f$ **splits over
$\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$**. In particular $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ is the splitting field
of the separable polynomial $f$, so $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q}$ is **Galois**,
of degree $[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha):\mathbb{Q}]=\deg f=6$. $\quad\checkmark$

### Part (c): $\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q})\cong S_3$

The group $G=\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q})$ has order $6$.
Each $\sigma\in G$ is determined by $\sigma(\alpha)$, which must be a conjugate
$\alpha\zeta^k$; since $|G|=6$, all six choices $k=0,\dots,5$ occur, giving
automorphisms $\sigma_k$ with $\sigma_k(\alpha)=\alpha\zeta^k$. Their effect on
$\zeta$ follows from $\zeta=\tfrac{1+\alpha^3}2$:
$$
\sigma_k(\zeta)=\frac{1+\sigma_k(\alpha)^3}{2}
=\frac{1+\alpha^3\zeta^{3k}}{2}
=\frac{1+(-1)^k\alpha^3}{2}
=\begin{cases}\zeta,& k\text{ even},\\[2pt]
1-\zeta=\zeta^{-1},& k\text{ odd},\end{cases}
$$
using $\zeta^3=-1$ and $1-\zeta=\bar\zeta=\zeta^{-1}$.

Take $\tau=\sigma_2$ and $\sigma=\sigma_1$. Then:
- $\tau(\alpha)=\alpha\zeta^2,\ \tau(\zeta)=\zeta$, so $\tau^k(\alpha)=\alpha
  \zeta^{2k}$ and $\tau^3=\mathrm{id}$: **$\tau$ has order $3$**;
- $\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha\zeta,\ \sigma(\zeta)=\zeta^{-1}$, so
  $\sigma^2(\alpha)=\sigma(\alpha\zeta)=\alpha\zeta\cdot\zeta^{-1}=\alpha$ and
  $\sigma^2(\zeta)=\zeta$: **$\sigma$ has order $2$**.

They do not commute:
$$
\sigma\tau(\alpha)=\sigma(\alpha\zeta^2)=\alpha\zeta\cdot\zeta^{-2}=\alpha\zeta^{-1},
\qquad
\tau\sigma(\alpha)=\tau(\alpha\zeta)=\alpha\zeta^2\cdot\zeta=\alpha\zeta^{3},
$$
and $\alpha\zeta^{-1}\neq\alpha\zeta^3$. So $G$ is the **non-abelian** group of
order $6$; explicitly $\sigma\tau\sigma^{-1}=\tau^{-1}$ (check on $\alpha$:
$\sigma\tau\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha\zeta^4=\tau^{-1}(\alpha)$), giving the dihedral
presentation
$$
G=\langle\sigma,\tau\mid \sigma^2=\tau^3=1,\ \sigma\tau\sigma^{-1}=\tau^{-1}
\rangle\cong S_3\ (\cong D_3).
$$
$\checkmark$

### Part (d): all subfields of $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$

Because $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q}$ is Galois with group $G\cong S_3$, the
**fundamental theorem of Galois theory** gives an inclusion-reversing bijection
$$
\{\text{subfields }M\text{ of }\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)\}
\ \longleftrightarrow\
\{\text{subgroups }H\le S_3\},\qquad M=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)^H,\ [M:\mathbb{Q}]=[G:H].
$$

**The subgroups of $S_3$ — exactly six.** By Lagrange a subgroup has order
$1,2,3,$ or $6$. Order $1$: $\{e\}$. Order $6$: $S_3$. Order $3$: a Sylow
$3$-subgroup; $n_3\equiv1\!\pmod3$ and $n_3\mid2$ force $n_3=1$, the unique
$A_3=\langle\tau\rangle$. Order $2$: generated by an element of order $2$, and
$S_3$ has exactly three such elements (the transpositions / the reflections
$\sigma_1,\sigma_3,\sigma_5$), giving three subgroups $\langle\sigma_1\rangle,
\langle\sigma_3\rangle,\langle\sigma_5\rangle$. Total $1+1+1+3=6$ subgroups, hence
**exactly six subfields**:

| subgroup $H$ | $|H|$ | $[M:\mathbb{Q}]$ | subfield $M=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)^H$ | Galois$/\mathbb{Q}$? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| $S_3$ | $6$ | $1$ | $\mathbb{Q}$ | yes |
| $A_3=\langle\tau\rangle$ | $3$ | $2$ | $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha^3)=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_6)$ | yes ($A_3\trianglelefteq S_3$) |
| $\langle\sigma_3\rangle$ | $2$ | $3$ | $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha^2)$ | no |
| $\langle\sigma_1\rangle$ | $2$ | $3$ | $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_3\,\alpha^2)$ | no |
| $\langle\sigma_5\rangle$ | $2$ | $3$ | $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_3^{2}\,\alpha^2)$ | no |
| $\{e\}$ | $1$ | $6$ | $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ | yes |

**Identifying the fixed fields.**

*Degree $2$ (fixed field of $A_3=\langle\tau\rangle$).* $\tau$ fixes $\zeta$, so
$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)\subseteq\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)^{A_3}$; and
$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta)=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha^3)$ has degree $2$
$=[S_3:A_3]$, so equality holds. Since $A_3\trianglelefteq S_3$, this quadratic
field is Galois over $\mathbb{Q}$ (as a quadratic field always is).

*Degree $3$ (fixed fields of the order-$2$ subgroups).* The element
$\beta=\alpha^2$ satisfies $\beta^3=\alpha^6=-3$, so $\beta$ is a root of
$x^3+3$, which is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ (Eisenstein at $3$); thus
$[\mathbb{Q}(\alpha^2):\mathbb{Q}]=3$ and $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha^2)$ is a degree-$3$
subfield. Its three conjugates are the fields generated by the three roots
$\alpha^2,\ \zeta_3\alpha^2,\ \zeta_3^2\alpha^2$ of $x^3+3$ (where
$\zeta_3=\zeta^2$). They are **distinct**: if two coincided, that field would
contain $\zeta_3$, hence the quadratic $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_3)=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$,
impossible inside a degree-$3$ field ($2\nmid3$). Being three distinct degree-$3$
subfields, they must be the three fixed fields of the three order-$2$ subgroups.
None is Galois over $\mathbb{Q}$: the corresponding order-$2$ subgroups are **not
normal** in $S_3$ (they are conjugate to one another), equivalently $x^3+3$ has
non-real roots so a single $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_3^j\alpha^2)$ does not contain all of
them. (For instance $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha^2)$ is the fixed field of
$\sigma_3:\alpha\mapsto\alpha\zeta^3=-\alpha$, since $\sigma_3$ fixes $\alpha^2$.)

**Completeness.** The list is complete because it is precisely the image of the
six subgroups of $S_3$ under the Galois correspondence, which is a bijection onto
the set of all subfields. Hence the subfields of $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ are exactly
$$
\mathbb{Q},\quad \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3}),\quad
\mathbb{Q}(\alpha^2),\ \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_3\alpha^2),\ \mathbb{Q}(\zeta_3^2\alpha^2),
\quad \mathbb{Q}(\alpha),
$$
six in all. $\quad\blacksquare$

### Remark

The engine is $\alpha^3=\sqrt{-3}$: a sixth root of $-3$ already encodes the
quadratic $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})=\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_6)$, so adjoining one root
adjoins them all. This is what makes $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ normal (unlike
$\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt[3]2)$ for $x^3-2$), with the same group $S_3$ but now realized
*inside a single radical field*. The subfield lattice is the subgroup lattice of
$S_3$ flipped over: one normal quadratic $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-3})$ (from the normal
$A_3$) and a conjugate triple of non-normal cubics
$\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_3^{\,j}\sqrt[3]{-3})$ (from the three reflections).
