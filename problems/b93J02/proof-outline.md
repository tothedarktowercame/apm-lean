## Imported Execution Notes

**Stage 1 — THE CLEAN PROOF**

**Why it’s hard.** The four headlines (PID, UFD, Noetherian, integrally closed) live in every algebra text, so it is tempting to assume they line up exactly. They do not. The first trap is that “UFD” is purely a factorization condition and does *not* force the chain conditions built into Noetherian rings; the infinite-polynomial example is easy to forget. The second trap is that Hilbert’s basis theorem only tells you a polynomial ring is Noetherian, not that it is a PID, so `k[x,y]` must be kept handy. Finally, the words “integrally closed” usually first appear in the context of Dedekind domains, so students often think they need class field theory; in reality the PID→UFD→integrally closed chain is just two textbook lemmas.

**Key insight.** Pull the story apart into two pieces: (a) a formal implication chain that is entirely handled by standard structure theorems, and (b) a set of classic counterexamples (`k[x,y]`, infinitely many variables, and `ℤ[√-5]`) that show the missing arrows really do fail. Once you picture the lattice PID ⊂ UFD ⊂ integrally closed and remember which hypotheses add Noetherianity, the whole problem becomes bookkeeping.

**Complete proof.**
1. *(I ⇒ II).* Let R be a PID. Every PID is a Euclidean domain in disguise, hence a GCD domain, hence a unique factorization domain; concretely, any nonzero ideal is generated by an element of minimal norm, so every element factors into irreducibles and associates are controlled by units. (This is Theorem 8.4 in standard algebra texts.)
2. *(II ⇒ IV).* Suppose R is a UFD with fraction field K and take α ∈ K integral over R. Write α = a/b with coprime a,b ∈ R (i.e. no irreducible divides both). The monic equation αᵐ + c_{m−1}α^{m−1}+⋯+c₀=0 with coefficients in R forces b to divide aᵐ, but unique factorization plus the coprimeness of a and b implies b is a unit. Hence α ∈ R, so R is integrally closed in K.
3. *(II ⇏ I).* Over a field k, the polynomial ring k[x,y] is a UFD (because adjoining indeterminates to a UFD preserves unique factorization), but the ideal (x,y) is not principal—no single polynomial generates both x and y, since any generator would have to divide them both. Therefore k[x,y] is not a PID.
4. *(II ⇏ III).* Take the polynomial ring k[x₁,x₂,x₃,…] in countably many indeterminates over a field. The same “adjoining indeterminates preserves UFD” lemma shows it is a UFD, but the chain of ideals (x₁) ⊊ (x₁,x₂) ⊊ (x₁,x₂,x₃) ⊊ ⋯ never stabilises, so the ring is not Noetherian.
5. *(III ⇏ I).* Return to k[x,y]. Hilbert’s basis theorem says polynomial rings in finitely many variables over Noetherian rings are Noetherian, so k[x,y] is Noetherian. We already observed that (x,y) is not principal, hence k[x,y] is not a PID.
6. *(Part (c)).* The classical example ℤ[√-5] = {a + b√-5 : a,b ∈ ℤ} is an integral domain in which 6 factors in two essentially different ways (6 = 2·3 = (1+√-5)(1-√-5)), so it is not a UFD.
