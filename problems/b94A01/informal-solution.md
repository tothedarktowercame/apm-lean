# APM b94A01: Classifying groups of order 10 as semidirect products ℤ/5 ⋊ ℤ/2

## Why It's Hard

This is a fully worked classification, and the difficulty is that it chains together every layer of the semidirect-product machine without skipping a step: compute $\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/5)$, count the homomorphisms $\mathbb{Z}/2\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/5)$, invoke the recognition theorem to force $G$ into the form $P\rtimes Q$, and then *compute* explicitly in the resulting nonabelian group. The conceptual spine is clean, but the bookkeeping is unforgiving: in parts (d)–(e) one must keep the conjugation relation $yxy^{-1}=x^{-1}$ straight and use it to push every $y$ to the right, reducing arbitrary words to the normal form $x^iy^j$. A single sign error in the exponent (working mod $5$) gives the wrong element. The payoff is that the *number* of homomorphisms (part b) equals the number of groups of order $10$ — exactly two, $\mathbb{Z}/10$ and $D_5$ — so the abstract count and the concrete classification coincide.

## The Key Insight

A group of order $10=2\cdot 5$ is **forced** to be a semidirect product $\mathbb{Z}/5\rtimes\mathbb{Z}/2$: the Sylow $5$-subgroup $P$ is normal (Sylow counting leaves $n_5=1$), $Q$ is a complement meeting it trivially, and $PQ=G$. The group is then completely determined by the conjugation action — a homomorphism
$$
\phi:Q=\mathbb{Z}/2\longrightarrow \operatorname{Aut}(P)\cong(\mathbb{Z}/5)^\times\cong\mathbb{Z}/4 .
$$
Since $\phi(y)$ must square to the identity, there are exactly **two** choices: the trivial map (giving the abelian $\mathbb{Z}/10$) and inversion $x\mapsto x^{-1}$ (giving the dihedral $D_5$). In the nonabelian case the single relation
$$
yxy^{-1}=x^{-1},\quad\text{equivalently}\quad yx^k=x^{-k}y,
$$
drives *all* arithmetic: push every $y$ rightward and reduce exponents mod $5$.

## Complete Proof

Let $|G|=10$, $P=\langle x\rangle$ a Sylow $5$-subgroup ($x^5=1$), $Q=\langle y\rangle$ a Sylow $2$-subgroup ($y^2=1$).

### Part (a): $\operatorname{Aut}(P)=\{\Psi_1,\Psi_2,\Psi_3,\Psi_4\}$, $\Psi_n(x^i)=x^{ni}$

$P\cong\mathbb{Z}/5$ is cyclic, so an automorphism $\Psi$ is determined by $\Psi(x)$, and $\Psi(x)$ must again be a **generator** of $P$. Since $5$ is prime, every non-identity element $x,x^2,x^3,x^4$ is a generator, giving the four candidates $\Psi_n(x)=x^n$ for $n=1,2,3,4$, extended multiplicatively by $\Psi_n(x^i)=x^{ni}$.

Each $\Psi_n$ is indeed an automorphism: it is a homomorphism, $\Psi_n(x^i x^j)=x^{n(i+j)}=\Psi_n(x^i)\Psi_n(x^j)$, and bijective with inverse $\Psi_m$ where $nm\equiv1\pmod 5$. The four are distinct (they differ on $x$), and there are no others (an automorphism must send $x$ to one of the four generators). Hence
$$
\operatorname{Aut}(P)=\{\Psi_1,\Psi_2,\Psi_3,\Psi_4\},\qquad \Psi_n\circ\Psi_m=\Psi_{nm\bmod 5},
$$
so $\operatorname{Aut}(P)\cong(\mathbb{Z}/5)^\times\cong\mathbb{Z}/4$ (cyclic of order $4$, generated by $\Psi_2$; $\Psi_1$ is the identity). $\qquad\checkmark$

### Part (b): exactly two homomorphisms $Q\to\operatorname{Aut}(P)$

A homomorphism $\phi:Q\to\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ is determined by $\phi(y)$, and since $y^2=1$ it must satisfy
$$
\phi(y)^2=\phi(y^2)=\phi(1)=\Psi_1\quad(\text{identity}).
$$
So $\phi(y)$ is an element of order dividing $2$ in $\operatorname{Aut}(P)\cong\mathbb{Z}/4$. Now $\Psi_n^2=\Psi_{n^2\bmod 5}=\Psi_1$ iff $n^2\equiv 1\pmod 5$, i.e. $n\equiv\pm1$, giving $n=1$ or $n=4$. Thus the only possibilities are
$$
\phi_1:\ y\mapsto\Psi_1\ \ (\text{trivial}),\qquad \phi_2:\ y\mapsto\Psi_4\ \ (x\mapsto x^{4}=x^{-1},\ \text{inversion}),
$$
and both are valid homomorphisms. So there are **exactly two**. $\qquad\checkmark$

### Part (c): $G$ is a semidirect product of $P$ by $Q$

**Recognition theorem (internal semidirect product).** *If $N\trianglelefteq G$, $H\le G$, $N\cap H=\{1\}$, and $NH=G$, then $G\cong N\rtimes_\phi H$, where $\phi:H\to\operatorname{Aut}(N)$ is the conjugation action $\phi(h)(n)=hnh^{-1}$.*

We verify the hypotheses with $N=P$, $H=Q$:

- **$P\trianglelefteq G$.** By Sylow, $n_5\equiv 1\pmod 5$ and $n_5\mid 2$; the only divisor of $2$ that is $\equiv 1\pmod 5$ is $1$, so $n_5=1$ and $P$ is the unique (hence normal) Sylow $5$-subgroup.
- **$P\cap Q=\{1\}$.** $|P\cap Q|$ divides $\gcd(5,2)=1$.
- **$PQ=G$.** $|PQ|=\dfrac{|P|\,|Q|}{|P\cap Q|}=\dfrac{5\cdot 2}{1}=10=|G|$.

All hypotheses hold, so $G\cong P\rtimes_\phi Q$ where $\phi:Q\to\operatorname{Aut}(P)$ is conjugation. By part (b), $\phi$ is one of two maps:
$$
\phi=\phi_1\Rightarrow G\cong\mathbb{Z}/5\times\mathbb{Z}/2\cong\mathbb{Z}/10\ (\text{abelian}),\qquad \phi=\phi_2\Rightarrow G\cong D_5\ (\text{nonabelian, dihedral of order }10).
$$
$\checkmark$

### Part (d): in the nonabelian case, $yx=x^4 y$

If $G$ is nonabelian, the conjugation action is nontrivial, so by (b) it is $\phi_2$: $y$ acts as $\Psi_4$, i.e.
$$
yxy^{-1}=\Psi_4(x)=x^{4}=x^{-1}.
$$
Since $y^2=1$ gives $y^{-1}=y$, multiply on the right by $y$:
$$
yx=x^{-1}y=x^{4}y .
$$
So $yx=\boxed{x^4 y}$, which is one of the listed elements. $\qquad\checkmark$

*(The element list in the statement, $\{1,x,x^2,x^3,x^4,y,xy,x^2y,x^3y,x^4y\}$, is the normal form $x^iy^j$ with $0\le i\le 4$, $0\le j\le 1$; the printed list appears to have dropped the element $x$.)*

### Part (e): $(x^2y)(x^3y)=x^4$

Use the relation from (d) in the form $yx^k=x^{-k}y$ (apply $yx=x^{-1}y$ repeatedly). To move the middle $y$ past $x^3$:
$$
yx^{3}=x^{-3}y=x^{2}y\qquad(\text{since }-3\equiv 2\bmod 5).
$$
Therefore
$$
(x^2y)(x^3y)=x^2\,(yx^{3})\,y=x^2\,(x^{2}y)\,y=x^{4}\,y^{2}=x^{4}\cdot 1=\boxed{x^{4}}.
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Remark — counting groups = counting actions

The whole problem is the slogan **"groups of order $pq$ are classified by the homomorphisms $\mathbb{Z}/q\to\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb{Z}/p)$."** Here the normal Sylow $5$-subgroup (forced by Sylow counting, since $5>2$) makes $G$ a semidirect product, and the two homomorphisms in part (b) are in bijection with the two isomorphism classes: the trivial action yields the cyclic $\mathbb{Z}/10$, and the inversion action yields the dihedral $D_5$. Parts (d)–(e) show the practical upshot: once you fix the action, every product collapses to normal form $x^iy^j$ by the rewriting rule $yx^k=x^{-k}y$ — the dihedral group is "the cyclic group $\langle x\rangle$ with a flip $y$ that reverses it." This is the same $\operatorname{Aut}$-counting mechanism that governs b01A02(b) (order $255$) and the general structure of metacyclic groups.
