# APM b95J02: Cyclic ⟺ size equals largest invariant factor — one PID theorem, two faces

## Why It's Hard

The two statements look like they belong to different worlds — one about finite abelian groups, the other about a linear operator on a vector space — and the temptation is to prove each from scratch with ad hoc bookkeeping. The real difficulty (and the intended insight) is recognizing that **they are the same theorem**, instances of the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain, applied to $R=\mathbb{Z}$ in (a) and $R=F[x]$ in (b). Once that dictionary is in place, both follow from one observation about invariant factors.

The subtle points are matching up the vocabulary:
- the "smallest $m$ with $g^m=1$ for all $g$" (the **exponent** of $G$) and the "least-degree monic $f$ with $f(T)=0$" (the **minimal polynomial** of $T$) are both the *largest invariant factor* — the generator of the annihilator of the module;
- "cyclic" (group generated by one element / $\{T^iv_0\}$ spanning $V$) means the module is **cyclic**, i.e. has a *single* invariant factor;
- the two "size" measures — $|G|$ and $\dim_F V$ — are the *product* of orders, resp. the *sum* of degrees, of the invariant factors.

The theorem says these sizes collapse to the size of the single largest factor exactly when there is only one factor.

## The Key Insight

Both objects are finitely generated **torsion modules over a PID** $R$, so the invariant-factor form of the structure theorem applies:
$$
M\cong R/(d_1)\oplus R/(d_2)\oplus\cdots\oplus R/(d_k),\qquad d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_k,\ \ d_i\ \text{non-units}.
$$
Three facts read off this decomposition prove everything:

1. **Largest factor = annihilator generator.** $d_k$ generates $\operatorname{Ann}_R(M)$: it is the unique (up to units) generator of $\{r\in R: rM=0\}$, because $\operatorname{lcm}(d_1,\dots,d_k)=d_k$ (the chain $d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_k$). In (a) $d_k$ is the exponent $m$; in (b) $d_k$ is the minimal polynomial.
2. **Cyclic $\iff k=1$.** $M$ is generated by one element iff $M\cong R/(d)$, i.e. a single invariant factor.
3. **Size $=$ "product/sum" over factors.** With $\operatorname{size}(R/(d)) = |d|$ (for $R=\mathbb{Z}$, the order) or $\deg d$ (for $R=F[x]$, the $F$-dimension), the total size is $\prod|d_i|$ resp. $\sum\deg d_i$.

Since each non-unit factor has $|d_i|\ge 2$ (resp. $\deg d_i\ge 1$), the total size equals the size of $d_k$ **iff** all other factors are trivial, i.e. **iff $k=1$, iff $M$ is cyclic**. That single argument is both theorems.

## Complete Proof

### General lemma (PID structure theorem, invariant-factor form)

Let $R$ be a PID and $M$ a finitely generated torsion $R$-module. Then
$$
M\cong R/(d_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus R/(d_k),\qquad d_1\mid d_2\mid\cdots\mid d_k,
$$
with each $d_i$ a non-unit, unique up to units (the *invariant factors*). Consequently $\operatorname{Ann}_R(M)=(d_k)$: indeed $r\,M=0$ iff $r$ kills each $R/(d_i)$ iff $d_i\mid r$ for all $i$ iff $\operatorname{lcm}(d_i)=d_k\mid r$. And $M$ is a **cyclic** module (i.e. $M=R m_0$ for some $m_0$, equivalently $M\cong R/(d)$) iff $k=1$.

### Part (a): a finite abelian group is cyclic iff $|G|$ equals its exponent

View $G$ as a finite (hence finitely generated torsion) $\mathbb{Z}$-module; $\mathbb{Z}$ is a PID. By the lemma,
$$
G\cong\mathbb{Z}/d_1\times\cdots\times\mathbb{Z}/d_k,\qquad d_1\mid\cdots\mid d_k,\ \ d_i\ge 2 .
$$
Let $m$ be the smallest positive integer with $g^m=1$ for all $g\in G$ (the exponent). By the lemma, $m\mathbb{Z}=\operatorname{Ann}_{\mathbb{Z}}(G)$, so $m=d_k$. Also $|G|=d_1 d_2\cdots d_k$.

**($\Rightarrow$)** If $G$ is cyclic, then $k=1$ and $G\cong\mathbb{Z}/d_1$, so $|G|=d_1=d_k=m$.

**($\Leftarrow$)** If $|G|=m$, then $d_1\cdots d_k=d_k$, hence $d_1\cdots d_{k-1}=1$. Since each $d_i\ge 2$, this forces $k=1$, so $G\cong\mathbb{Z}/d_1$ is cyclic.

(In general $m=d_k$ divides $|G|=d_1\cdots d_k$, with equality precisely when $k=1$.) Thus $G$ is cyclic $\iff |G|=m$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

### Part (b): $T$ acts cyclically iff $\dim V$ equals the degree of the minimal polynomial

Make $V$ an $F[x]$-module by letting $x$ act as $T$: $f(x)\cdot v:=f(T)v$. Then $V$ is finitely generated over $F[x]$ (already finite-dimensional over $F\subseteq F[x]$) and torsion (by Cayley–Hamilton, the characteristic polynomial annihilates $V$, so every $v$ is killed by a nonzero polynomial). $F[x]$ is a PID, so by the lemma
$$
V\cong F[x]/(f_1)\oplus\cdots\oplus F[x]/(f_k),\qquad f_1\mid\cdots\mid f_k\ \ \text{(monic, non-constant)} .
$$
Two identifications:

- **Minimal polynomial $=f_k$.** The least-degree monic $f$ with $f(T)=0$ is the monic generator of $\operatorname{Ann}_{F[x]}(V)$, which by the lemma is $(f_k)$. So $\mu_T=f_k$ and $\deg\mu_T=\deg f_k$.
- **Dimension $=\sum\deg f_i$.** Since $\dim_F F[x]/(f)=\deg f$, we have $\dim_F V=\sum_{i=1}^k\deg f_i$.
- **Cyclic action $\iff$ cyclic module.** "$T$ acts cyclically" means some $v_0$ has $\{T^iv_0:i\ge0\}$ spanning $V$, i.e. $F[x]\cdot v_0=V$ — exactly that $V$ is a cyclic $F[x]$-module, i.e. $k=1$.

**($\Rightarrow$)** If $T$ acts cyclically, $k=1$, so $V\cong F[x]/(f_1)$ and $\dim_F V=\deg f_1=\deg f_k=\deg\mu_T$.

**($\Leftarrow$)** If $\dim_F V=\deg\mu_T=\deg f_k$, then $\sum_{i=1}^k\deg f_i=\deg f_k$, so $\sum_{i<k}\deg f_i=0$. Since each $\deg f_i\ge 1$, this forces $k=1$, hence $V\cong F[x]/(f_1)$ is cyclic; taking $v_0$ to be the image of $1\in F[x]$, the powers $T^iv_0$ span $V$, so $T$ acts cyclically.

(In general $\deg\mu_T=\deg f_k\le\sum\deg f_i=\dim V$, with equality precisely when $k=1$.) Thus $T$ acts cyclically $\iff \dim V=\deg\mu_T$. $\qquad\blacksquare$

### Remark — one theorem, the $R=\mathbb{Z}$ and $R=F[x]$ specializations

The proofs are word-for-word parallel because they *are* one proof under the dictionary "finite abelian group $=$ torsion $\mathbb{Z}$-module" and "$(V,T)=$ torsion $F[x]$-module (with $x\rightsquigarrow T$)":

| concept | $R=\mathbb{Z}$, part (a) | $R=F[x]$, part (b) |
|---|---|---|
| size of $R/(d)$ | order $\lvert d\rvert$ | dimension $\deg d$ |
| total size | $\lvert G\rvert=\prod\lvert d_i\rvert$ | $\dim V=\sum\deg d_i$ |
| largest invariant factor $d_k$ | exponent $m$ | minimal polynomial $\mu_T$ |
| cyclic | cyclic group | $T$ acts cyclically (cyclic vector) |

The shared content is that the largest invariant factor controls *both* the annihilator and (only when alone) the size: $\operatorname{size}(M)=\operatorname{size}(R/(d_k))\iff k=1\iff M$ cyclic. In (b) this is the classical fact that $T$ is *non-derogatory* (cyclic vector exists) iff its minimal and characteristic polynomials coincide — the operator-theoretic shadow of "exponent equals order."
