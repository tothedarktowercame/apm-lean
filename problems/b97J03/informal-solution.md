# APM b97J03: How many conjugates land in ℚ(α) — the normalizer index r = [N_G(H):H]

## Why It's Hard

The quantity $r$ — "how many of the $n$ roots of $P$ already live in the field
$\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ generated by one of them" — looks like it should depend on
fine arithmetic of $P$, but it is governed entirely by the **subgroup geometry**
of the Galois group. The whole problem is to translate "$\beta\in\mathbb{Q}
(\alpha)$" into group theory. The clean translation identifies the roots with
cosets $G/H$ (where $H=\operatorname{Stab}_G(\alpha)$), and then a root $g\alpha$
lies in $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)=L^H$ **iff $g$ normalizes $H$** — so $r=[N_G(H):H]$,
which divides $[G:H]=n$. The traps: (a) you must know the action on roots is
transitive (irreducibility) and use orbit–stabilizer; the divisibility is then
the tower law, not a number-theoretic miracle. (b) "abelian" trivializes the
normalizer ($N_G(H)=G$), equivalently makes $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)/\mathbb{Q}$
normal, so *all* roots come along. (c) For $x^6-2$ the subtlety is that
$\mathbb{Q}(2^{1/6})$ is **real**, so it can only hold the real roots $\pm2^{1/6}$
— giving $r=2$, a concrete case of $r<n$ because $G$ (here $\cong D_6$) is
non-abelian.

## The Key Insight

Let $L$ be a splitting field of $P$, $G=\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q})$, and
$H=\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q}(\alpha))=\operatorname{Stab}_G(\alpha)$. Since
$P$ is irreducible (and separable, char $0$), $G$ acts **transitively** on the
$n$ distinct roots, and orbit–stabilizer gives the bijection
$$
\{\text{roots of }P\}\ \xleftrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\ G/H,\qquad
g\alpha\ \leftrightarrow\ gH,\qquad |G/H|=n .
$$
A root $g\alpha$ lies in $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)=L^H$ iff $H$ fixes it, i.e.
$g^{-1}Hg\subseteq H$, i.e. (finite groups) $g\in N_G(H)$. Hence the roots inside
$\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ are exactly the cosets in $N_G(H)/H$:
$$
\boxed{\,r=[N_G(H):H]\,}
$$
and since $H\le N_G(H)\le G$, the tower law $[G:H]=[G:N_G(H)]\,[N_G(H):H]$ forces
$r\mid n$. If $G$ is abelian then $N_G(H)=G$, so $r=[G:H]=n$. For $x^6-2$, $H$ is
generated by complex conjugation and $[N_G(H):H]=2$, i.e. $r=2$.

## Complete Proof

**Setup.** Let $L\subseteq\mathbb{C}$ be a splitting field of $P$ over
$\mathbb{Q}$ and $G=\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q})$. As $\operatorname{char}
\mathbb{Q}=0$, $P$ is separable, so it has $n=\deg P$ **distinct** roots
$\alpha=\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n\in L$, and $G$ permutes them. Because $P$ is
**irreducible**, this action is **transitive**: for any root $\alpha_i$ there is
$g\in G$ with $g\alpha=\alpha_i$ (any two roots of an irreducible polynomial are
related by an automorphism of the splitting field). Put
$$
H=\operatorname{Stab}_G(\alpha)=\{g\in G:g\alpha=\alpha\}
=\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)),
$$
so that, by the Galois correspondence, $L^H=\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$.

**Roots $\leftrightarrow$ cosets.** The map $gH\mapsto g\alpha$ is well defined
and injective ($g\alpha=g'\alpha\iff g^{-1}g'\in H\iff gH=g'H$) and, by
transitivity, surjective onto the set of roots. Hence it is a bijection
$$
G/H\ \xrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\ \{\text{roots of }P\},\qquad
\#\{\text{roots}\}=[G:H]=n .
$$
(In particular $[G:H]=n$, recovering $|H|=|G|/n$.)

### Part (a): $r\mid n$

By definition $r$ is the number of roots lying in $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)=L^H$. A
root $g\alpha$ lies in $L^H$ iff every $h\in H$ fixes it:
$$
h(g\alpha)=g\alpha\ \ \forall h\in H
\iff (g^{-1}hg)\alpha=\alpha\ \ \forall h\in H
\iff g^{-1}hg\in H\ \ \forall h\in H
\iff g^{-1}Hg\subseteq H .
$$
For a finite group, $g^{-1}Hg\subseteq H$ implies $g^{-1}Hg=H$ (both have the same
order), which says exactly $g\in N_G(H)$, the normalizer of $H$. Moreover this
condition depends only on the coset $gH$ (if $g\in N_G(H)$ then $gH\subseteq
N_G(H)$). Therefore the bijection above restricts to
$$
\{\text{roots of }P\text{ in }\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)\}
\ \xleftrightarrow{\ \sim\ }\
N_G(H)/H,
$$
giving
$$
r=[N_G(H):H].
$$
Since $H\le N_G(H)\le G$, the tower law gives
$[G:H]=[G:N_G(H)]\cdot[N_G(H):H]$, i.e. $n=[G:N_G(H)]\cdot r$. Hence
$$
\boxed{r\mid n.}
$$
$\checkmark$

### Part (b): $G$ abelian $\Rightarrow r=n$

If $G$ is abelian then every subgroup is normal, so $N_G(H)=G$. By part (a),
$$
r=[N_G(H):H]=[G:H]=n .
$$
$\checkmark$

*Equivalent field-theoretic phrasing.* $G$ abelian $\Rightarrow H\trianglelefteq
G\Rightarrow \mathbb{Q}(\alpha)=L^H$ is **Galois** (hence normal) over
$\mathbb{Q}$. A normal extension that contains one root $\alpha$ of the
irreducible $P$ contains *all* its roots (the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$,
namely $P$, splits in any normal extension containing $\alpha$). So all $n$ roots
lie in $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$, i.e. $r=n$.

### Part (c): $r=2$ for $P(x)=x^6-2$

$P=x^6-2$ is irreducible over $\mathbb{Q}$ by **Eisenstein at $p=2$**, so $n=6$.
Choose the real root $\alpha=2^{1/6}>0$; the full set of roots is
$$
2^{1/6}\zeta^k\quad(k=0,1,\dots,5),\qquad \zeta=\zeta_6=e^{2\pi i/6},
$$
since $(2^{1/6}\zeta^k)^6=2\cdot\zeta^{6k}=2$.

**Direct count.** The field $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)=\mathbb{Q}(2^{1/6})\subseteq
\mathbb{R}$ is **real**, so it can contain only the real roots of $x^6-2$. The
real sixth roots of $2$ are exactly $2^{1/6}$ and $-2^{1/6}$ (the polynomial
$x^6-2$ has two sign-change real roots; the other four roots
$2^{1/6}\zeta^k$ with $k=1,2,4,5$ are non-real). Both real roots lie in
$\mathbb{Q}(2^{1/6})$: $2^{1/6}=\alpha$ and $-2^{1/6}=2^{1/6}\zeta^3$ (as
$\zeta^3=-1$). Hence
$$
\boxed{r=2.}
$$

**Cross-check via the formula $r=[N_G(H):H]$.** The splitting field is
$L=\mathbb{Q}(2^{1/6},\zeta_6)$. Here $[\mathbb{Q}(2^{1/6}):\mathbb{Q}]=6$ and
$\zeta_6\notin\mathbb{R}\supseteq\mathbb{Q}(2^{1/6})$ while
$[\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_6):\mathbb{Q}]=2$, so $[L:\mathbb{Q}(2^{1/6})]=2$ and
$|G|=[L:\mathbb{Q}]=12$ (the group is $\cong D_6$, dihedral of order $12$ — in
particular **non-abelian**, so part (b) does not apply and we should expect
$r<n$). Then $|H|=|G|/n=12/6=2$, and the nontrivial element of
$H=\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb{Q}(2^{1/6}))$ must fix the real $2^{1/6}$ while
moving $\zeta_6$ — it is **complex conjugation** $\tau$, with $\tau(\zeta_6)=
\zeta_6^{-1}$. A root $2^{1/6}\zeta^k$ is fixed by $\tau$ iff
$$
2^{1/6}\zeta^{-k}=2^{1/6}\zeta^{k}\iff \zeta^{2k}=1\iff 3\mid k\iff k\in\{0,3\},
$$
so again $r=2$ (the roots $2^{1/6}$ and $-2^{1/6}$). Consistently
$[N_G(H):H]=2$, i.e. $|N_G(H)|=4$. $\quad\blacksquare$

### Remark

The content of the problem is the identity $r=[N_G(H):H]$: the number of
conjugates of $\alpha$ falling into $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ measures how far $H$ is
from being normal. It is largest ($r=n$) exactly when $\mathbb{Q}(\alpha)$ is
already normal over $\mathbb{Q}$ — guaranteed when $G$ is abelian — and smallest
when $H$ is **self-normalizing** ($N_G(H)=H$, giving $r=1$, so $\alpha$ is the
*only* root in its own field). The example $x^6-2$ sits in between: $H=\langle
\text{complex conjugation}\rangle$ has normalizer of order $4$, so precisely
$r=2$ of the six roots — the two real ones — lie in $\mathbb{Q}(2^{1/6})$.
