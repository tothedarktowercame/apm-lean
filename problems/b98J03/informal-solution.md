# APM b98J03: The Primitive Element Theorem and Steinitz's criterion — simple ⟺ finitely many intermediate fields

## Why It's Hard

This is the full primitive-element story in four moves, and each move uses a
*different* mechanism, so the trap is reaching for the wrong one. Part (a) is not
Galois theory at heart — it is the fact that the multiplicative group of a finite
field is **cyclic**, and a generator of $K^\times$ is automatically a primitive
element. Part (b) is the classic counting/pigeonhole argument that needs $k$
**infinite** (so that $\{x+ty:t\in k\}$ is an infinite family) played against
"finitely many subfields"; the delicate step is extracting $y\in F$ from
$k(x+t_1y)=k(x+t_2y)$ by using that $t_1-t_2$ is an *invertible* scalar. Parts
(c)–(d) are the converse engine (Steinitz): (c) pins each intermediate field $E$
to a concrete invariant — the coefficients of the minimal polynomial $g$ of $x$
over $E$ — and the crux is showing $E$ is *recovered* from those coefficients via
a degree count; (d) then bounds the number of fields by the number of **monic
divisors of $f=\min(x,k)$ in $K[T]$**, which is finite. Throughout, the silent
hazard is conflating the polynomial variable with the generator $x$; below the
variable is $T$.

## The Key Insight

Two complementary facts about a **simple** extension $K=k(x)$, with
$f(T)=\min(x,k)\in k[T]$:

- **(c) Recovery.** For $k\subseteq E\subseteq K$, if $g(T)=\min(x,E)$ then $E$
  equals the field $E_0=k(\text{coeffs of }g)$ generated by $g$'s coefficients.
  Reason: $g$ is irreducible over the *subfield* $E_0$ too, so
  $[K:E_0]=\deg g=[K:E]$ while $E_0\subseteq E$ — forcing $E_0=E$.
- **(d) Finiteness.** Each such $g=\min(x,E)$ is a **monic divisor of $f$ in
  $K[T]$** (because $f(x)=0$ puts $f\in E[T]$, so $g\mid f$). The map
  $E\mapsto\min(x,E)$ is injective by (c), and $f$ has only finitely many monic
  divisors in $K[T]$ — hence finitely many intermediate fields.

For existence of a primitive element: over a **finite** $k$, take a generator of
the cyclic group $K^\times$ (a); over an **infinite** $k$ with finitely many
subfields, maximize $[k(x):k]$ and use pigeonhole on $k(x+ty)$ (b). Parts (b) and
(d) together are Steinitz: *$K/k$ is simple iff it has finitely many intermediate
fields.*

## Complete Proof

Let $[K:k]<\infty$. We write $T$ for the polynomial indeterminate (reserving $x$
for elements of $K$), and $\min(\alpha,F)$ for the monic minimal polynomial of
$\alpha$ over a field $F$.

### Part (a): $k$ finite — the group is cyclic, and $K$ is simple

Since $k$ is finite and $[K:k]<\infty$, $K$ is a **finite field**, say $|k|=q$
and $[K:k]=n$, so $|K|=q^n$. The extension $K/k$ is Galois (finite fields are
perfect and finite extensions are normal), and
$$
\operatorname{Aut}_kK\ \cong\ \mathbb{Z}/n\quad\text{is \textbf{cyclic}},
$$
generated by the Frobenius automorphism $\phi(a)=a^{q}$. *(No proof required.)*

For simplicity, recall that the multiplicative group $K^\times$ of any finite
field is **cyclic**. Let $x$ be a generator of $K^\times$. Then every nonzero
element of $K$ is a power of $x$, so $k(x)$ contains $0$ and all powers of $x$,
i.e. $k(x)=K$. Hence $K=k(x)$ is a **simple (primitive) extension**.
$\quad\checkmark$

### Part (b): $k$ infinite, finitely many intermediate fields $\Rightarrow$ $K$ simple

Among all elements of $K$, choose $x\in K$ with $[k(x):k]$ **maximal** (possible:
these degrees are positive integers bounded by $[K:k]$). We claim $k(x)=K$.

Suppose not; pick $y\in K\setminus k(x)$. As $t$ ranges over the **infinite** set
$k$, consider the intermediate fields $k(x+ty)$. By hypothesis there are only
finitely many fields between $k$ and $K$, so by pigeonhole two distinct scalars
$t_1\neq t_2$ in $k$ yield the *same* field:
$$
F:=k(x+t_1y)=k(x+t_2y).
$$
Then $F$ contains both $x+t_1y$ and $x+t_2y$, hence their difference
$(t_1-t_2)y\in F$. Since $t_1-t_2\in k^\times$ is invertible (and $k\subseteq F$),
we get $y\in F$, and then $x=(x+t_1y)-t_1y\in F$ as well.

Thus $x,y\in F=k(x+t_1y)$. In particular $k(x)\subseteq F$, and the inclusion is
**strict** because $y\in F$ but $y\notin k(x)$. Writing $w=x+t_1y\in K$, we have
$F=k(w)$ with
$$
[k(w):k]=[F:k]>[k(x):k],
$$
contradicting the maximality of $[k(x):k]$. Therefore no such $y$ exists, i.e.
$k(x)=K$: the extension is **simple**. $\quad\checkmark$

### Part (c): $E=k(\text{coefficients of }g)$, where $g=\min(x,E)$

Assume $K=k(x)$, and let $k\subseteq E\subseteq K$ with $g(T)=\min(x,E)\in E[T]$.
Let $E_0=k(c_0,\dots,c_{d-1})$ be the subfield of $K$ generated over $k$ by the
coefficients of $g(T)=T^d+c_{d-1}T^{d-1}+\cdots+c_0$. Since each $c_j\in E$, we
have $k\subseteq E_0\subseteq E$.

By construction $g(T)\in E_0[T]$ and $g(x)=0$, so $x$ is algebraic over $E_0$.
Moreover $g$ is irreducible over $E$ (it is a minimal polynomial), hence
irreducible over the **subfield** $E_0\subseteq E$: a nontrivial factorization in
$E_0[T]$ would be one in $E[T]$. Therefore $g$ is also the minimal polynomial of
$x$ over $E_0$, giving $[E_0(x):E_0]=\deg g$ and likewise $[E(x):E]=\deg g$.

Now $x$ generates $K$ over $k$, hence over any intermediate field: from
$K=k(x)\subseteq E_0(x)\subseteq K$ we get $E_0(x)=K$, and similarly $E(x)=K$.
Hence
$$
[K:E_0]=[E_0(x):E_0]=\deg g=[E(x):E]=[K:E].
$$
With $E_0\subseteq E$ and the tower law $[K:E_0]=[K:E]\,[E:E_0]$, this forces
$[E:E_0]=1$, i.e.
$$
E=E_0=k(\text{coefficients of }g).
$$
$\checkmark$

### Part (d): a simple extension has finitely many intermediate fields

Assume $K=k(x)$ and let $f(T)=\min(x,k)\in k[T]$. Let $E$ be any field with
$k\subseteq E\subseteq K$, and $g_E(T)=\min(x,E)\in E[T]$.

**$g_E$ is a monic divisor of $f$ in $K[T]$.** Indeed $f\in k[T]\subseteq E[T]$
and $f(x)=0$, so the minimal polynomial $g_E$ of $x$ over $E$ divides $f$ in
$E[T]$; as $E[T]\subseteq K[T]$, this same factorization holds in $K[T]$. Thus
$g_E$ is a monic divisor of $f$ in $K[T]$.

**There are only finitely many such divisors.** Factor $f=p_1^{e_1}\cdots
p_r^{e_r}$ into monic irreducibles in the UFD $K[T]$. Every monic divisor of $f$
in $K[T]$ has the form $p_1^{a_1}\cdots p_r^{a_r}$ with $0\le a_j\le e_j$, so
there are at most $\prod_j(e_j+1)<\infty$ of them.

**The assignment $E\mapsto g_E$ is injective.** By part (c), $E$ is recovered from
$g_E$ as $E=k(\text{coefficients of }g_E)$. So distinct intermediate fields give
distinct minimal polynomials.

Combining, the number of intermediate fields is at most the number of monic
divisors of $f$ in $K[T]$, which is finite:
$$
\#\{\,E: k\subseteq E\subseteq K\,\}\ \le\ \prod_{j=1}^r(e_j+1)\ <\ \infty.
$$
$\blacksquare$

### Remark

Parts (b) and (d) are the two halves of **Steinitz's theorem**: a finite
extension $K/k$ is simple **iff** it has only finitely many intermediate fields.
The forward direction (d) is structural and characteristic-free — each
intermediate field is the "field of coefficients" of a divisor of $\min(x,k)$
inside $K[T]$, so the subfield lattice injects into the (finite) divisor lattice
of one polynomial. The converse (b) needs $k$ infinite for the linear sweep
$x+ty$; the finite case is handled separately in (a) by the cyclicity of
$K^\times$. Specializing (b) to the case "$K/k$ separable" (where finiteness of
intermediate fields is automatic, since they correspond to the finitely many
subgroups of the Galois group of a normal closure) recovers the usual
**Primitive Element Theorem**: every finite separable extension is simple.
