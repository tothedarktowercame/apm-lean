# APM b03J02: (a, x) is not principal, and K[x,y] is not a PID

## Why It's Hard

The statement of part (a) feels self-evident — "$(a,x)$ obviously needs two generators" — but that intuition is not a proof. To show *non*-principality you must **rule out every conceivable single generator $d$**, and the only way a single $d$ could fail is ruled out by a precise interplay of all three hypotheses: $R$ is a *domain* (so degrees add and a divisor of a constant is a constant), $a$ is *nonzero* (so $a$ is a genuine constant of degree $0$), and $a$ is *non-invertible* (the punchline, reached by evaluating at $x=0$). Drop any one hypothesis and the claim can fail — e.g. if $a$ were a unit, then $(a,x)=R[x]=(1)$ is principal.

Part (b)'s difficulty is a **change of perspective** rather than a calculation. One must see the two-variable ring $K[x,y]$ as a *one-variable* polynomial ring $K[y][x]$ over the coefficient domain $R=K[y]$, and then realize that $R=K[y]$ — although not a field — supplies exactly the raw material part (a) needs: a nonzero non-invertible element, namely $y$. Spotting that "peel off one variable" reduction is the whole game.

## The Key Insight

A principal ideal $(d)\subseteq R[x]$ is *rigid* about degrees. If $(d)$ contains both the degree-$0$ element $a$ and the degree-$1$ element $x$, then $d$ divides both:

- $d\mid a$ with $a$ a nonzero constant forces $\deg d=0$, i.e. $d\in R$ (degrees add in a domain);
- $d\mid x$ with $d\in R$ forces $d$ to be a **unit** (matching the coefficient of $x$).

But a unit generator gives $(d)=R[x]\ni 1$, and writing $1=p\,a+q\,x$ and setting $x=0$ makes $a$ invertible — contradiction. The two generators $a$ (degree $0$) and $x$ (degree $1$) are *irreconcilable* into one. For (b): $K[x,y]=K[y][x]$ is a polynomial ring over the domain $K[y]$, which is **not a field**, so it contains a nonzero non-unit ($y$) to feed into (a). At bottom, PIDs have Krull dimension $\le 1$, while $K[x,y]$ has dimension $2$.

## Complete Proof

### Part (a): $(a,x)$ is not principal in $R[x]$

Let $R$ be an integral domain and $a\in R$ nonzero and non-invertible. Suppose, for contradiction, that
$$
(a,x)=(d)\qquad\text{for some }d\in R[x].
$$
Recall that since $R$ is a domain, degrees are additive: $\deg(fg)=\deg f+\deg g$ for nonzero $f,g\in R[x]$ (leading coefficients multiply to a nonzero element).

**$d$ is a nonzero constant.** Since $a\in(d)$, we have $d\mid a$, say $a=d\,e$ with $e\in R[x]$. As $a\ne 0$, both $d,e\ne 0$, and $\deg d+\deg e=\deg a=0$, forcing $\deg d=0$. Thus $d\in R\setminus\{0\}$.

**$d$ is a unit.** Since $x\in(d)$, we have $d\mid x$, say $x=d\,f$ with $f\in R[x]$. Here $\deg f=1$; write $f=c_1x+c_0$ with $c_i\in R$. Then
$$
x=d\,f=(dc_1)x+(dc_0)\ \Longrightarrow\ dc_1=1,
$$
so $d$ is invertible in $R$, i.e. $d\in R^\times$.

**Contradiction via evaluation.** A unit generator gives $(d)=R[x]$, so $1\in(a,x)$: there exist $p,q\in R[x]$ with
$$
1=p(x)\,a+q(x)\,x .
$$
Evaluating the ring homomorphism "set $x=0$" ($R[x]\to R$),
$$
1=p(0)\,a+q(0)\cdot 0=p(0)\,a,
$$
which exhibits $a$ as invertible in $R$ with inverse $p(0)$ — contradicting that $a$ is non-invertible. Hence no such $d$ exists, and $(a,x)$ is **not principal**. $\qquad\blacksquare$

### Part (b): $K[x,y]$ is not a PID

Let $K$ be a field. Regard
$$
K[x,y]=K[y][x]=R[x],\qquad R:=K[y],
$$
the polynomial ring in the single variable $x$ over the coefficient ring $R=K[y]$. Now:

- $R=K[y]$ is an **integral domain** (a polynomial ring over a field has no zero divisors).
- $a:=y\in R$ is **nonzero** and **non-invertible**: the units of $K[y]$ are exactly the nonzero constants $K^\times$, and $y$, having degree $1$ in $y$, is not a constant.

These are precisely the hypotheses of part (a). Applying it with this $R$ and $a=y$, the ideal
$$
(y,\ x)\ \subset\ R[x]=K[x,y]
$$
is **not principal**. A ring possessing a non-principal ideal is by definition not a PID, so $K[x,y]$ is not a PID. $\qquad\blacksquare$

### Remark — what really fails, and the dimension viewpoint

The non-principal ideal $(x,y)$ is the maximal ideal of polynomials vanishing at the origin $(0,0)$; that it cannot be generated by a single polynomial is the algebraic reflection of the geometric fact that a point in the plane is a codimension-$2$ locus, not a hypersurface. More structurally, part (a) is the engine behind the general principle that **$R[x]$ is a PID if and only if $R$ is a field**: a PID has Krull dimension $\le 1$, and feeding a nonzero non-unit $a\in R$ into the construction $(a,x)$ produces a height-$2$ ideal $(a,x)\supsetneq(a)\supsetneq(0)$ (a chain of primes when $a$ is prime) that no principal ideal can be. Part (b) is the case $R=K[y]$: since $K[y]$ is not a field, $K[y][x]=K[x,y]$ inevitably fails to be a PID — indeed it has dimension $2$. (It remains a UFD, however — the failure is principality, not unique factorization.)
